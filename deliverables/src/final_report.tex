\section{Problem and Our Answer}
\textbf{The brief.} Design a digital experience that Grade 11-12 (Intermediate) students from underserved communities in Hyderabad \emph{want} to use, and that teaches four pillars: \emph{plan and budget}, \emph{save and manage}, \emph{understand investing}, \emph{stay financially safe}. It starts with about 1,500 students delivered through NGOs and must scale. It is judged on seven measures (simple, relatable, persona-led, language-ready, game-based, intuitive UI, NGO-integrated) under one guardrail: \emph{educate, do not recommend investments; simulations are assumptions, not promises}.

\textbf{Our reading.} Financial literacy here means sensible decisions with one's \emph{own} money: pocket money, a scholarship landing in a first bank account, UPI on a shared phone, a first salary. It is not finance as a subject and not stock trading. The two easy answers both miss: a quiz app with coins is a textbook with confetti, and a trading simulator breaks the guardrail and is far from a 16-year-old's life.

\textbf{Our answer.} A browser game in which the student lives a compressed life (Intermediate student, first salary, earning more, then a look back at 60) and every money decision is a Pok\'emon-style \textbf{money battle} at a Hyderabad place they know. Two claims hold throughout: \textbf{the game teaches the concepts while being relatable to the target demographic}, and \textbf{the teaching is done through short abstract animations} that play \emph{after} the choice and its consequence, never as a lecture first.

\textbf{The teaching loop.} \emph{Situation} (a task from the student's difficulty bucket, framed for their persona) $\rightarrow$ \emph{choice} (2-4 moves) $\rightarrow$ \emph{consequence} (wallet and happiness bars move now; some effects are scheduled months later) $\rightarrow$ \emph{the Professor walks in}: one line of praise for a good choice, or a lesson line plus a 10-20\,s abstract animation for a weak one $\rightarrow$ the concept goes into a \emph{mistake bank} and comes back later in a new disguise until it is answered right. Weak choices send the player down a detour; falling for a scam, running out of money or happiness at zero restarts the task. Good choices can also be \emph{shown} paying off: pick \rs20 chips over a \rs300 biryani and the next day you bat three balls at the turf; order the biryani and the next scene is the match you cannot afford. Money comes from life (allowance, scholarship, wages), not from quests; mini-games are framed as side gigs and lock while a bar is low.

\textbf{Personas and difficulty are separate.} Four picture questions (where money comes from, weekly spend as a range, own bank account, whose phone has UPI) pick one of three personas: \emph{pocket money}, \emph{scholarship} or \emph{earner}. The persona changes only the story: the same scam is an ``OTP sent by mistake'' on a parent's phone, a fake scholarship officer asking \rs500, or a fake payment screenshot at the shop counter. The quiz score alone picks \emph{simple}, \emph{medium} or \emph{hard}.

\textbf{End of game.} The epilogue at 60 shows what savings and the four made-up assets became, next to a cash box at home. A \emph{what if} screen replays the single biggest mistake from the decision log with the other choice, on the same random seed, side by side (shown only to the student). The after-quiz asks the same five concepts in new situations, giving a learning gain that is not memorisation. The student gets an NCFE-aligned score and a safety score, and a PDF report goes to the NGO facilitator with the guardian in CC.

\section{Approach: Why This Design}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prices feel like the student's own life.} Stage 1 always gives \rs5{,}000 in-game a month, and in-game price $=$ real price $\times$ \rs5{,}000 $/$ the student's monthly pocket money (from the indirect weekly-spend band). A \rs200 cricket bat costs \rs200, \rs400 or \rs1{,}000 in-game for students with \rs5{,}000, \rs2{,}500 or \rs1{,}000 a month: the same \emph{share} of their money as in real life. Stages 2-3 pay rent, food and travel (55\% and 60\% of income) every month.
  \item \textbf{Consequence before explanation.} The bars move first, then the Professor explains; the lesson lands on a decision the student owns. Animations are shapes and on-screen text only (a six-operation DSL in JSON), so translating one means swapping strings.
  \item \textbf{Real life is delayed.} 45 delayed or repeating effects: a ``no-cost EMI'' bites every month, money lent to a friend does not come back, skipped savings meet an emergency with no buffer. Happiness rises with an impulse buy and falls a month later when the student cannot afford the turf match.
  \item \textbf{Local analogies, not imported ones.} Compounding is the Pillalamarri banyan, whose roots become trunks (Hyderabad has no snowballs); diversification is the Charminar vendor who sells umbrellas \emph{and} water bottles.
  \item \textbf{The guardrail is designed in.} Only made-up assets and fictional companies, never a ticker or a ``buy''; every projection carries ``assumes X\% a year; real returns vary and can be negative''. The Charminar Market scores \emph{behaviour only} (diversified, held through the crash, refused the hot tip, low churn); money made is shown but never scored.
  \item \textbf{Private by default.} A nickname is the only identifier. The game never asks for OTP, PIN, Aadhaar or a student's phone or email; the facilitator enters report contacts with consent on setup. The sponsor sees aggregates only.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Architecture.} One generic battle screen driven by JSON, in four parts with one-way dependencies: \emph{shared} contract types and a zod schema; a \emph{pure engine} in TypeScript (seeded RNG, economy scaler, chapter loop, task selector with pillar rotation and an investing gate, consequence scheduler, mistake bank, investing and market simulations, NCFE scoring, deterministic what-if replay, save and resume); a \emph{content pack} that non-coders and NGOs can edit (tasks per pillar $\times$ difficulty with one framing per persona, detours, events, explainers, places, strings per language); and a \emph{Phaser 3 client} shipped as an offline PWA. A small Node service emails the report and falls back to a local outbox offline; a static dashboard mocks the NGO view.

\section{Results}
\textbf{Engine correctness.} \textbf{151 unit tests passing} in 19 files: engine 77 (RNG, scaler, effects, selector, mistake bank, hints, investing, market, scoring, what-if determinism, save and resume), content 30, mini-games 20, client logic 19, quiz 4, report 1. Typecheck, lint and content validation run on every change.

\textbf{Content validation.} 24 main tasks (12 / 6 / 6 per stage), 72 persona framings, 22 detours, 17 random events, 17 concepts each with an explainer, 45 delayed or repeating effects, 422/422 voice-over lines matched to their clips; every NCFE item (17/17) is evidenced by at least one choice; every cross-reference resolves; a double-blind string check passes.

\textbf{Scripted players on the real content} (20 seeds per difficulty; ranges are across simple, medium and hard). \emph{Good} always picks the best move, \emph{random} picks at random, \emph{worst} picks the worst move but never repeats a fatal one, so it can finish. All 9 balance targets are met.

Good play ends solvent, happy and NCFE-literate; careless play detours about three times and ends with little to spare; the worst play restarts often and goes into debt, yet always finishes. At M2, careless play still ended rich; monthly living costs in Stages 2-3 fixed that.

\textbf{Play tests.} Two unguided team play tests on a phone and in phone mode (02:15 and 06:10) logged 36 issues, each with an owner; 25 were closed by 08:10, including all 12 from the second test.% TODO: update the count at 10:30
\ They changed the design: a tighter economy, the Professor as the one teaching character, a labelled happiness number, a heading on every quiz screen, longer mini-game timers (60\,s and 45\,s), re-worded after-quiz questions, a report PDF that explains why each number matters, the facilitator setup path, and a sound toggle that works everywhere. A request for a stock simulator became the Charminar Market, reshaped to respect the guardrail.

\textbf{Devices and network} (production build in headless Chrome, 360$\times$640, Fast 3G with a 4$\times$ CPU throttle, time until the title screen is drawn): \textbf{21.0\,s} on a first visit (1.1\,MB, mostly many small sprite and backdrop files), \textbf{1.2\,s} on a repeat visit and \textbf{1.9\,s} for an offline reload through the service worker. The first visit got slower since M2 (5.1\,s) as the map, characters and backdrops landed; after one load on Wi-Fi the game runs offline, so a facilitator can prepare phones before a session.
\textbf{Report pipeline:} a seeded game produces the PDF report, which reaches a sandbox inbox with the guardian in CC; with no network it is saved locally and offered as a download.

\textbf{Limitations and next steps.} Learning gain with real students is not yet measured: the next step is a pilot with one NGO batch, with the dashboard live. Prices and situations are team estimates and need a 10-student survey; Telugu and Hindi need a native-speaker review and their own recorded voice-over (Bhashini for more languages); the first visit on a slow network needs packed sprite atlases and a smaller first download.
